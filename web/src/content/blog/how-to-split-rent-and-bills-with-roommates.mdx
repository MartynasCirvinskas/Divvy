Living with roommates is great — until someone buys toilet paper three times in a row and starts keeping a mental tally, or the electricity bill arrives and nobody can agree on who owes what. Money conversations are awkward at the best of times. In a shared flat, they happen every month.

This article lays out a practical system for splitting rent and household bills with roommates. It's for people who want to be fair, avoid resentment, and stop doing mental arithmetic every time someone goes to the supermarket.

## The two problems with splitting costs by hand

Most roommates start with the best intentions and a rough system: "we'll take turns buying things and it'll even out." That works for about three weeks. Then someone goes abroad for a month, someone else's partner starts staying over four nights a week, and suddenly "it'll even out" means different things to different people.

The second problem is debt accumulation. If you're tracking who owes what informally, small amounts pile up. By the time you try to settle, nobody can remember what the numbers were based on. Feelings get involved.

A good system has two properties: it's transparent (everyone can see exactly what's owed and why) and it's low-friction (logging an expense takes 10 seconds, not 10 minutes of scrolling through bank statements).

## How to handle the regular bills

Rent is usually simple: it's the same amount every month, split however you've agreed. If all rooms are the same size, an equal split makes sense. If one bedroom is noticeably larger, a percentage split is fairer. EvenJar supports both — and you can mark rent as a recurring expense so it auto-logs each month rather than requiring one person to remember to enter it every time.

For utilities (electricity, gas, internet), equal splits are usually the right call. Usage-based splits are theoretically fairer but practically impossible to calculate accurately — and they cause arguments about who had the heating on. Equal split, move on.

Council tax and insurance are the same: log them once, recurring if they're monthly, equal split.

## How to handle irregular shared costs

This is where informal systems break down. Cleaning supplies, bin bags, a shared streaming subscription, the occasional group dinner — these are irregular and easy to forget to log.

The habit to build is: log it when you buy it. Open EvenJar, enter the amount, pick who it's for. Thirty seconds. If you're shopping for the whole flat and only some things are shared, enter the shared items separately.

For costs that only affect some people — say, one roommate buys a desk lamp that lives in the common area — use a custom split to log just that roommate's share or split it only among the people who agreed to it.

## Tips for keeping it fair long-term

**Agree on the rules upfront.** Is a partner who stays over regularly expected to contribute to utilities? Are groceries shared or separate? Write it down (even in the group's expense notes) so there's no ambiguity later.

**Log expenses the same day.** The longer you wait, the more likely you are to forget the amount or the context. Receipts help, but the habit of logging in the moment is better.

**Do a monthly check-in.** Take five minutes at the end of each month to review balances together before settling. It catches any missed entries and keeps everyone on the same page.

**Don't let balances build up.** If someone's balance is growing month over month, address it before it becomes a big number with feelings attached. Small, regular settlements are easier than one large reckoning.
